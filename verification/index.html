Session 1 · The verification block

Three questions.
Then decide.

You just saw how a fluent, confident, wrong claim slips past the instincts you'd normally trust. Here's what to actually do about it — a short framework you can run on any AI-generated claim in about ten seconds. Not a rule that says never trust the model. A way to decide, claim by claim, how much verification effort the situation actually calls for.

The three questions

01

Would the model know this cold, or is it improvising?

Rule of thumb: Well-attested, widely documented facts are reliable. Exact numbers, precise dates, quantities, and citations are exactly where fluent guessing happens.
02

Can you check it against a primary source in under two minutes?

Rule of thumb: If checking is fast, there's no excuse not to. If it would take real digging, that itself is information — the claim is expensive to verify and risky to trust blind.
03

What happens if you're wrong?

Rule of thumb: A wrong claim in a casual draft is a typo. A wrong claim in a policy document, a legal memo, or a grant application is a different category of problem entirely.

Run it on the errors you just saw

Two of the three errors from the previous exercise, walked through the framework.

The number
"12 hours and 36 minutes" on the lunar surface
Would the model know this cold, or is it improvising?
Improvising. An exact duration down to the minute is a specific figure, not something reliably "known" the way a well-attested fact is.
Can you check it in under two minutes?
Yes, fast. One search confirms the actual figure is 21 hours, 36 minutes.
What happens if you're wrong?
Depends on the room. Low stakes in a casual conversation. Higher if this becomes a fact sheet, a teaching slide, or gets repeated by someone else as true.
Verdict: Specific and fast to check — verify it every time. There's no real cost to looking, and a meaningful cost to being fluently wrong in front of a room.
The citation
"Turner and Mahoney (2003)" — a paper that doesn't exist
Would the model know this cold, or is it improvising?
Improvising. Citations are exactly the kind of specific, checkable claim models fabricate with total fluency — plausible authors, plausible year, well-formatted.
Can you check it in under two minutes?
Yes — if you actually try. A citation search takes under a minute. Either the paper exists or it doesn't. The check is fast; the temptation to skip it is the real risk.
What happens if you're wrong?
High. A fabricated citation in anything that leaves your desk — a report, a grant application, a syllabus — is a professional liability, not just an error.
Verdict: This is the highest-value check in the whole framework. Never let a citation into a document you haven't independently verified.

What this shows

You don't need to verify everything. You need to know which things to verify.

Running every AI-generated sentence past a fact-checker is not realistic, and it is not the point. The three questions exist to help you triage in seconds: specific, checkable, and consequential claims get verified. Vague, unverifiable, or low-stakes claims don't need the same effort.

Notice what the framework does not ask: it never asks whether the model sounded confident. Confidence is not a signal. It's the thing this whole exercise exists to un-learn.

The two claims from the previous exercise both landed in the same place — verify it, every time — because both were specific, fast to check, and costly if wrong. That combination is common. It is also exactly the combination our instincts are worst at catching, because fluent text gives no outward sign of which claims fall into it.

For facilitators

Using this in Session 1 · after the 1:15 mark

  • This is the direct answer to the question find-the-error leaves open. If you ran that exercise, the bridge line was "so what do we actually do about it" — this page is the "do."
  • Project the three questions first, without the applied examples. Let the room sit with the framework for a moment before showing it in action. The framework should feel obvious in hindsight, not clever.
  • The applied cards are click-to-reveal. Good for pacing — ask the room to guess the verdict before you click "Walk through it."
  • "Try it yourself" works best live, together. Read the scenario out loud, ask for a show of hands on each question before revealing the next one, then compare the room's collective verdict to the gauge.
  • The closing point is the one to say out loud: the framework never asks whether the model sounded confident. That's deliberate — confidence is exactly the signal this whole session has been teaching people not to trust.
