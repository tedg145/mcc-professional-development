01Write it the way you actually would

A real, recurring situation — the way you'd normally describe it to a colleague, name and all. This box is never scanned and never leaves your screen. Its only job is giving you something to compare against once you build the safe version.

02Build the version you could actually use

Rewrite it so a stranger reading it couldn't identify the student — not just the name, but the combination of details. Then check it.

    ▶ How the checker works

    It isn't AI, and it isn't a real statistical disclosure tool — it's a transparent, rule-based scan for eight kinds of detail that tend to narrow a description down to one person, the same categories the "major + GPA + term" example already points at. Each category it finds adds points; the total sets the verdict.

    DetailWeightWhy it's risky
    Program or major2Narrows the pool fast in a small program
    Class standing / term1Pins the timeframe
    GPA or academic standing2A number is more identifying than a description
    A specific date2"Recently" carries the same information you need
    A rare or unusual circumstance3The single most identifying thing you can include
    A 4+ digit number3Reads as a student or case ID
    Text in quotation marks2A verbatim quote is a fingerprint
    A possible name4Defeats the exercise outright

    Honest limit: this is a teaching heuristic, not a compliance check. A green score means "worth a second look," not "cleared" — which is why Step 3 still puts a second person on it, whatever the checker says.

    03The adversarial round

    Running this in a session: trade your green- or yellow-scored hypothetical with a neighbor, without your own context. Their job is to guess who it is, or say plainly they can't. If they can guess — even with a hedge, even "probably" — it's not safe yet, whatever the checker said. Working solo? Ask yourself the same question about anyone you already know is in that program this term.

    04Keep it

    The pattern matters more than this one example. Name it before you move on.

    ▶ Teaching notes — running this in a session

    Where it goes

    Track C (Advising & Student-Facing Roles), after the sole-possession trap has already landed in Tier 1 Session 3. This lab is where that idea turns into a habit instead of a fact people nod at.

    Demo the pair before anyone tries it solo

    Load the worked example on the projector. Run the risky first draft, let the room see it score red, then click through to the generalized version and watch it go green. Seeing the same situation twice — once risky, once safe — is more persuasive than any explanation of why.

    The score is a floor, not a ceiling

    A green score means the obvious patterns are gone, not that the hypothetical is safe. Step 3 is not optional — it's the part that catches what a room this small could still recognize, which no rule list can know. Say this out loud before anyone starts Step 3, or people will treat the score as the answer.

    Pair people who don't share a program

    If you can arrange it, pair an advisor with someone from a different department for the swap. Someone in the same small program as the real student is exactly who's most likely to guess right — and exactly who most needs to see that they can.

    What "I can't guess" is worth

    A partner who genuinely can't guess is doing real verification work, not being polite. If partners are guessing correctly more often than not, that's the signal to slow down and spend longer on Step 2 as a group before anyone moves on.
