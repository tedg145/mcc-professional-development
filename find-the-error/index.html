What this shows

The pattern is almost always the same. And the answer is almost always the number.

Most rooms catch the substituted name (the ship). Some rooms catch the wrong number. Almost no room catches the fabricated citation. That order is not an accident. It maps directly to how much a person's existing knowledge helps them verify each type of claim.

Ship names, birthdates, capital cities — when the model gets these wrong, someone in the room usually remembers the right answer. Numbers, times, quantities, and citations are different. Nobody carries the exact figure in their head. Nothing in the sentence signals "wrong." The model produces all three types of claim with identical fluent confidence, and the room's error-detection instincts — trained on humans — have nothing to work with.

The citation is the most instructive of the three. An AI-generated fake citation looks more like a real citation than most real citations do. Real papers have messy titles, missing years, weird punctuation, first authors nobody remembers. Fake ones are clean, plausible, well-formatted. The polish is the tell — but only if you know to look for it.

For facilitators

Using this in Session 1 · the 0:50–1:15 block

  • Project the paragraph and give the room 60–90 seconds to read. Don't rush this. The paragraph is short but the exercise depends on people actually reading it, not scanning.
  • Ask "which of these are wrong?" before revealing. Hands up, quick tally per suspicion. This commits people to a position before the answer — commitment is what makes the reveal land.
  • Hit "Show me" to reveal without individual clicks. Useful when projecting to a room that isn't clicking on their own devices. All three errors light up amber, and the per-error explanations appear underneath.
  • The teaching moment is "which was hardest?" Ask it out loud after the reveal. The answer is always the number, or the citation. Watch how the room's certainty about the wrong ship falls apart when they realize they only caught it because they happened to know Endeavor was a Space Shuttle.
  • The bridge to the next block: "So if the model produces confident, well-formatted, plausible-sounding wrong information — and our human instincts don't catch it — what are we supposed to do?" That's the setup for the verification block that follows.
  • Hand out the QR: bottom-left button, or Shift + Q. Lets the room retry on their own devices, or take it back to their department.
