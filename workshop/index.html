What changed

The bottleneck moved.

Writing software used to require knowing syntax. That was the wall, and it kept almost everyone out. It isn't the wall anymore. The wall now is knowing what to build, and knowing what will go wrong — and you already have the first one.

01

Describe

You say what you want in ordinary language. "A form where staff request a room, and I see all the requests in one list."

02

Get something working

The tool writes it, runs it, and shows you. You look at it, say what's wrong, and it changes. That loop is the whole job.

03

Be responsible for it

This is the part nobody teaches. It's real software now, on the real internet, possibly holding real data. That's what most of today is about.

The skill is no longer typing code. It's judgment about what you've made.

Part one

What you actually need.

There are two roads. One needs nothing but a browser. The other runs on your own machine and gives you more power for more setup. Start on the first road — everyone does.

Road one · start here

The browser builders

You open a tab, type what you want, and a working app appears. Nothing to install, nothing to configure, and they will publish it to a real web address for you. This is where your first thirty minutes should go — and where a locked-down work laptop leaves you no choice anyway.

Road two · later today

Agents on your own machine

Tools like Claude Code run on your computer, read and write real files, and run real commands. Far more capable, and the only option once a project gets serious. It needs installing, a paid plan, and a little comfort with a terminal.

Task 1

Ship something in thirty minutes.

Pick one real, small, annoying thing from your actual job. Not a grand idea — a small one. A form. A calculator. A checklist that emails you a summary.

  1. Open bolt.new. You will be asked to sign in — no credit card needed.
  2. Describe the tool in three or four sentences. Say who uses it and what they see.
  3. When it appears, do not accept the first version. Tell it one specific thing that's wrong. Then another.
  4. Click Deploy. Copy the URL. Send it to the person next to you and have them use it.
30 minutes · you leave this step with a working link
Part two

Your laptop is already a server.

Every website you've used lives on someone else's computer. Running something "locally" means it's running on the machine in front of you, and your browser is fetching it from that same machine. Nothing crosses the internet at all.

The analogy that works: localhost is your office phone extension. From inside the building you dial 3000 and it rings. Nobody outside the building can dial your extension directly — they'd need the main number first. That's not a bug. That's the whole design.

You, on your laptop

localhost:3000 works perfectly. The request leaves your browser, loops straight back into your own machine, and never touches the network card.

Your director, on hers

✕

She types the same address and reaches her own machine, which is running nothing. 127.0.0.1 means "me" on every computer on earth. There is no version of it that means your laptop.

This is the single most common confusion for first-timers. You build something, it works beautifully, you paste the link into Teams — and everyone reports an error. Nothing is broken. The link is meaningless outside your machine. Getting a shareable address means deploying, which is the next section.

What running it locally looks like

Terminal — room-booker

Two things trip everyone up. The terminal is now occupied — that isn't frozen, that's the server running; open a second window for anything else. And Ctrl+C stops it, at which point the site instantly stops working.

The three things to install

One

Node.js 24 LTS

Version 24.19.0 as of August 2026 — take the LTS, not the newest. This is the engine that runs the code. From nodejs.org, default installer.

Two

Git

Track Changes for a whole folder, with an undo that reaches back months. From git-scm.com. Add GitHub Desktop if you'd rather click than type.

Three

VS Code

Where you look at the files. Free. You will spend less time here than you expect — the agent does most of the typing.

Before you plan on any of this: check whether your college laptop lets you install software at all. Many staff machines don't. If yours doesn't, the browser tools aren't the easy path — they're the only path.

Part three

Getting it on the internet, for free.

Free hosting is real, not a trial, and not a trick. But each of the big four free tiers has one specific edge that cuts, and they're all different edges.

The concept

A static site is a folder of finished files

Handed over exactly as they sit on disk. No thinking per visitor. It's a printed handout in a rack by the door — anyone can take one, the rack does no work, and ten thousand copies cost almost nothing. That's why it's free.

The limit

You outgrow it the moment anything differs per person

Users logging in. A form whose contents must be stored. Anything you must keep secret from the visitor. Then you need a dynamic site — a librarian who looks up your record and prints you a custom page. Someone has to be on duty, and duty costs money.

Task 2

Get a real address.

  1. Push the thing you built in Task 1 to a GitHub repository. Use GitHub Desktop if the command line isn't your thing.
  2. Connect that repository to Cloudflare Pages. Accept every default.
  3. Wait ninety seconds. You now have a public HTTPS address anyone on earth can reach.
  4. Sit with that for a second. Anyone on earth can reach it. That's the next section.
20 minutes · you leave this step live on the internet
Part four

There is no such thing as a secret in a web page.

Not minified. Not obfuscated. Not base64-encoded. Not fetched from a file with an unguessable name. Right-click, View Source — or open the Network tab and watch every request the page makes, then replay it with different values.

Any key in your front-end is published

If it shipped to the browser, treat it as compromised and rotate it. Deleting the file does nothing — it's in your Git history and possibly someone's cache.

Hiding a button hides nothing

Client-side checks are a courtesy to polite users. Disabling a field stops the field, not the request. Every real check must happen where the user cannot reach it.

So only publish what is safe to publish

That's not a limitation — it's the design. It's why the next section matters more than anything else today.

Two words people use interchangeably, and shouldn't

Authentication

Who are you?

The ID check at the door. Login screens, passwords, magic links, SSO. This part almost always works.

Authorization

What are you allowed to see?

Which rooms the badge opens. This is the part that's missing in nearly every breach you'll read about — everyone logged in fine, and then could read everyone else's records.

Logging in is not the same as being limited.

Put a gate on it while it's young

A prototype's security isn't "probably fine" — it's unknown. A password in front of the whole site converts an unknown-risk public asset into a known-audience internal one. It buys you the right to be wrong.

Task 3

Lock the door you just opened.

  1. Put Cloudflare Access in front of the site you deployed in Task 2.
  2. Add your own email, and the email of the person next to you.
  3. Open it in a private window. Confirm you're stopped.
  4. Have someone not on the list try. Confirm they're stopped too.

Part five · the most important thirty minutes

When you add a database,
the browser talks to it directly.

This is the part that surprises people. In the standard no-code stack, your site doesn't ask a server which asks the database. The visitor's own browser asks the database. That's what makes a free static site behave like a real app — and it's why one particular setting is not optional.

Safe to publish

The publishable key

Formerly called the anon key. It is not a password — it's a return address that tells the database which project you mean. It has to be in the browser, because in this design the browser is the client. Supabase lists it as safe to expose.

Never in a browser

The secret / service_role key

It carries BYPASSRLS — every rule you write simply does not apply to it. If an error got fixed by swapping in this key, you didn't fix the error. You removed the lock.

So the entire security model rests on one sentence: the public key gets you to the door, and row-level security decides what's behind it. With RLS off, that public key stops being a return address and becomes a master key you have published on the internet.

Try it. Flip the switch.

This is a simulation — no real database, no real data. The behavior is accurate.

RLS is off. Anyone with the key — which is in your page source — can read every row. They can also insert, update and delete. On the free tier you keep one day of logs, so you will not be able to prove what happened.

What RLS actually is

A normal database permission is a key to a filing cabinet — you're either in or out. Row-level security is a rule that says you may open this cabinet, but you only ever see the folders with your name on them. The rule lives in the cabinet, not in the app. That's the part that matters: the app cannot talk its way around it, because the check happens inside the database after the request arrives.

The seven ways it goes wrong

01

The table was made in SQL

Tables made in the dashboard's Table Editor get RLS automatically. Tables made in the SQL editor do not — and SQL is exactly what an AI assistant writes. This is the big one.

02

USING (true)

A policy that means "everyone, always." It satisfies every "do you have a policy?" checkbox while providing zero protection. It is the most dangerous mistake because it looks like security.

03

The table you added last month

Month one was locked down. The table added in month three has no policy. Nobody re-audits.

04

The service key in the browser

Someone hit a permissions error and "fixed" it by swapping keys. It works instantly, which is exactly why it's so seductive.

05

A security-definer view

Runs with its creator's permissions, not the caller's. RLS is on, the policy is right, and data still leaks straight through the view.

06

RLS on, no policy — and panic

This blocks everything, which is the correct behavior. But it looks like "my app is broken," and the fix people reach for is turning RLS off. That panic is the actual attack path.

07

USING without WITH CHECK

USING is which rows you may touch. WITH CHECK is what you may turn them into. It matters the moment the rows you may read are broader than the rows you may write — omit it and Postgres silently reuses USING, so anyone who can see a row can rewrite it.

Two sentences to take home: open Database → Security Advisor. If it isn't clean, you are not live. The advisors run automatically and email organization owners a weekly summary — it is free, it is already on, and almost nobody opens it.

Task 4

Break your neighbour's app.

  1. Add a Supabase database to your project and put two or three fake records in it. Fake. Invent them.
  2. Turn RLS on. Write one policy so a user sees only their own rows.
  3. Open Database → Security Advisor. Fix everything it flags. Re-run until clean.
  4. Now swap laptops. Your job is to see data you shouldn't. Open DevTools, find the key, try the URL.
  5. Whatever you find, tell them. That's the exercise.
25 minutes · nobody's survives the first time
One more thing that will bite this class specifically. Supabase pauses free projects after about a week of inactivity. You'll build something today, come back in two weeks, and find it dead. You didn't break it — click Resume. But put a weekly reminder in your calendar, and export anything you care about before a long break. The free tier has no automatic backups.
Part six

If you're thinking about selling it.

Start here, before anything about payments or pricing. This is the question almost nobody asks, and it is the only decision on this page that is genuinely hard to reverse.

Who owns what you build? MCC has a posted intellectual property policy — E-XVII, effective February 2011. It says employees have exclusive rights to what they develop on their own time without substantial institutional resources. It also says anything developed using substantial institutional resources is jointly owned by the College and you. "Substantial" is not defined anywhere in the policy.
The trap

"Jointly owned" is not a compromise

People hear it and think "we split the money." Under default copyright rules, joint owners each hold an undivided interest — but neither can grant an exclusive license or sell the whole thing without the other. A jointly-owned product is effectively unsellable and un-fundable without the College's signature, and revenue carries a duty to account.

The other regime

It isn't only an IP question

Texas Penal Code § 39.02 makes it abuse of official capacity for a public servant to misuse government property or services for personal benefit, graded by value. Building a commercial product on the college laptop, on college time, with college data isn't just a policy problem.

What to do instead, and it is not complicated. Personal device. Personal accounts. Personal software licenses. Nights and weekends. No institutional data, ever — not even scrubbed, not even for testing. And if there's any ambiguity, get a written determination before you build, not after you have a customer. Under E-XVII the path runs supervisor → VP of Instruction & Student Engagement → President.

The policy also requires disclosure within 60 days after notification of marketability. And note it dates to 2011 — it says nothing about cloud services, institutional software licenses, or the AI tools the College provides. Expect ambiguity; don't expect it to resolve in your favor.

Then the ordinary business questions

Payments

Stripe, or a middleman

Stripe is 2.9% + 30¢ and every tax obligation stays yours. A merchant-of-record — Paddle, Lemon Squeezy — charges about 5% and becomes the legal seller, which means they register and remit sales tax everywhere. You're paying roughly two points for a compliance department. For a solo project that's cheap.

Texas sales tax

SaaS is taxable here

Texas treats it as a data processing service: 6.25% state plus up to 2% local, but only 80% of the price is taxable — so an effective ceiling of 6.6%. And because you live in Texas, there's no economic-nexus safe harbor. The obligation starts at your first Texas sale.

Structure

An LLC is one layer, not a shield

$300 to form in Texas. It protects against contract debts — but not against your own torts, which is exactly what a solo software business gets sued for. Pair it with a limitation-of-liability clause and insurance, or it's doing less than you think.

The privacy law that actually applies to you. California's CCPA has thresholds a side project will never hit — unless it sells personal data, which has no threshold at all. The Texas Data Privacy and Security Act has no revenue or user-count threshold at all — it applies based on doing business in Texas. Small businesses are exempt from most of it, but not from the rule against selling sensitive personal data without explicit consent. And you need a posted privacy policy the moment you collect an email address.

Treat it as skill acquisition with a lottery ticket attached. The skills transfer to your day job with certainty. The revenue does not.

Do the IP analysis before you write code — it's free and it's the only irreversible step. Defer the LLC, the trademark and the tax registrations until there's actual revenue. The most common failure is spending three months and $2,000 on formation and branding for a product that never gets a customer.

Before you make anything public

The gate.

Work down it. If you can't personally explain what stops a stranger from reading your database, it is not ready.

The final question, and it's the only one that matters: assume everything in this app becomes public tomorrow. Are you comfortable? If the answer is no, the answer isn't better security — it's different data.
What happens next

You have a live URL and something that works.

That already puts you ahead of most people who've thought about building something. Three things worth doing this month, in order.

01

File a Tool Charter

One page, five questions, ten minutes. What does it do, what data does it touch, who else uses it, who owns it when you leave, and what happens when it's wrong.

02

Show someone

A tool nobody else uses is a hobby. A tool one colleague depends on is infrastructure — and needs an owner, documentation and a fallback.

03

Come to the open lab

Monthly, no agenda, all tools on the table. Bring the thing that broke. Something always breaks.
