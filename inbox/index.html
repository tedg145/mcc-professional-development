The Inbox Hour
Eight messages, one Tuesday morning

It is 8:05 and there are eight things waiting. Most of them are ordinary. Deciding what to do is the skill — the drafting is the easy part, and it is the part that gets people into trouble when the deciding was skipped.

Tuesday · 8:05 AM

Open a message on the left. Read it properly before you decide — that is the whole exercise.

▶ Teaching notes — running this in a session

Why four of the eight are ordinary

If every message were a trap, the lesson would be distrust everything, and people who distrust everything stop using the tools and stop reporting problems. Half of this inbox is genuinely routine and genuinely fine to hand over. The skill being practised is telling the difference, and that requires real examples of both.

Run it silently first

Ten minutes, no talking, everyone on their own laptop. Then go through the four consequential ones as a group. The disagreement in the room is more instructive than the feedback text, and people argue harder about a decision they have already committed to.

The one nobody expects

The message with instructions buried inside it catches almost everyone, including technical staff. The point is not that people are careless — it is that a document can carry text addressed to the assistant rather than to you, and a tool reading it on your behalf may act on it. Anything that arrives from outside is data, not instructions.

Where it fits

After the five patterns and the data line. It works as the practical follow-on to Getting Your Time Back, and it pairs naturally with The Delegation Line — that scenario teaches the rule, this one applies it under time pressure.

The close

End on the routine ones. People leave remembering the traps; the actual message is that most of the morning was safe to delegate, and knowing which part was not is what earns you the rest.
